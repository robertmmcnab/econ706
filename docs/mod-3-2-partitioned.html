13  Partitioned Regression

13.1 Introduction

Understanding partitioned regression is an element of discussing the properties of the least squares regression model. Omitting relevant variables or variables that are correlated with other independent variables influences the least squares parameter estimates. The coefficients from the multiple regression model are partial regression coefficients.

13.2 Orthogonal Estimates

If the matrix of independent variables can be partitioned into two orthogonal matrices, then the partitioned least squares estimates are:

\[\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{b_1} \\ \boldsymbol{b_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1} & \boldsymbol{0} \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2} \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{X_1^T y} \\ \boldsymbol{X_2^T y} \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{b_1} \\ \boldsymbol{b_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & (\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2})^{-1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{X_1^T y} \\ \boldsymbol{X_2^T y} \end{bmatrix}\] \[\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{b_1} \\ \boldsymbol{b_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{(X_1^T X_1)^{-1}X_1^T y} \\ \boldsymbol{(X_2^T X_2)^{-1}X_2^T y} \end{bmatrix}\]

Since the \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are orthogonal then \((\boldsymbol{X^T X})\) matrix is block diagonal.

Since \((\boldsymbol{X^T X})\) matrix is block diagonal, the least squares estimates of the coefficients for the first set of independent variables from the regression of \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) are the same as the least squares estimates obtained from the regression of \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\).

This, however, is not the usual case.

13.3 Non-Orthogonal Partitions

It is more likely that the partition of the matrix of independent variables will create two non-orthogonal matrices.

If \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are non-orthogonal, then the Orthogonal Partitioned Regression Theorem no longer applies. In other words, we cannot regress \(\boldsymbol{y}\) separately on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) and obtain the same coefficients that we would obtain by regressing \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X}\).

Start with the partitioned normal equations:

\[\begin{matrix} (1) \\ (2) \end{matrix} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1} & \boldsymbol{X_1^T X_2} \\ \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1} & \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{b_1} \\ \boldsymbol{b_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{X_1^T y} \\ \boldsymbol{X_2^T y} \end{bmatrix}\]

From the partitioned normal equations, we can solve (1) for \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\).

\[(\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1}) \boldsymbol{b_1} + (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_2}) \boldsymbol{b_2} = \boldsymbol{X_1^T y}\]

\[\boldsymbol{b_1} = (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T y} - (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2}\]

\[\boldsymbol{b_1} = (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2})\]

The coefficients \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\) are obtained from the regression of \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) minus a correction vector

The regression of \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\):

\[(\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{y}\]

The correction vector is:

\[(\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2}\]

What is the correction vector?

The correction vector is the regression of the variables (done separately and put together in a matrix) in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) on all the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\).

The correction vector will be zero if and only if the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are orthogonal.

The second term \(\boldsymbol{b_2}\) will be zero if the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) have no influence on \(\boldsymbol{y}\), that is, the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are irrelevant to \(\boldsymbol{y}\).

We can only omit \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) if the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are irrelevant.

If we omit relevant variables, then the parameter estimates in \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\) are affected.

If \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are not orthogonal, then the parameter estimates in \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\) are affected.

13.4 Deriving Estimates

What if \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are not orthogonal? Start with the partitioned normal equations:

\[\begin{matrix} (1) \\ (2) \end{matrix} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1} & \boldsymbol{X_1^T X_2} \\ \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1} & \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{b_1} \\ \boldsymbol{b_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{X_1^T y} \\ \boldsymbol{X_2^T y} \end{bmatrix}\]

From the partitioned normal equations, we can solve (1) for \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\):

\[\boldsymbol{b_1} = (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2})\]

From the partitioned normal equations, we can use (2) to obtain: \[(\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1}) \boldsymbol{b_1} + (\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2}) \boldsymbol{b_2} = \boldsymbol{X_2^T y}\]

Substituting \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\) into the above yields: \[(\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1}) (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2}) + (\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2}) \boldsymbol{b_2} = \boldsymbol{X_2^T y}\] \[(\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1}) (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{y} - (\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1}) (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2} + (\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2}) \boldsymbol{b_2} = \boldsymbol{X_2^T y}\]

We can collect like terms to obtain:

\[\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2} + \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2} = \boldsymbol{X_2^T y}\]

\[\boldsymbol{X_2^T X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2} - \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{X_2} \boldsymbol{b_2} = \boldsymbol{X_2^T y} - \boldsymbol{X_2^T X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T} \boldsymbol{y}\] \[[\boldsymbol{X_2^T}(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T}) \boldsymbol{X_2} ]\boldsymbol{b_2} = \boldsymbol{X_2^T}(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T}) \boldsymbol{y}\]

Now solve for \(\boldsymbol{b_2}\) \[\boldsymbol{b_2} = [\boldsymbol{X_2^T}(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T}) \boldsymbol{X_2} ]^{-1}[\boldsymbol{X_2^T}(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T})\ \boldsymbol{y}]\]

13.5 Residual Maker

Previously, we derived the residual maker matrix, M:

\[\boldsymbol{I - X(X^T X)^{-1}X} = \boldsymbol{M}\]

Remember that \(\boldsymbol{M}\) is the residual maker, that is, \(\boldsymbol{My} = \boldsymbol{e}\).

In other words, \(\boldsymbol{My}\) makes the residuals for the regressions of \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X}\).

The counterpart of M when for the partition \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) is:

\[\boldsymbol{I - X_1(X_1^T X_1)^{-1}X_1} = \boldsymbol{M_1}\]

This means that \(\boldsymbol{M_1}\) is the residual maker for regressions on the \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) variables.

\(\boldsymbol{M_1 y}\) are the residuals from regressing \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on the \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) variables.

\(\boldsymbol{M_1 X_2}\) are the column by column residuals from regressing each variable in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) on all the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\).

We can use \(\boldsymbol{M_1}\) to simplify:

\[\boldsymbol{b_2} = [\boldsymbol{X_2^T}(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T}) \boldsymbol{X_2} ]^{-1}[\boldsymbol{X_2^T}(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{X_1} (\boldsymbol{X_1^T X_1})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X_1^T})\ \boldsymbol{y}]\]

\[\boldsymbol{b_2} = [\boldsymbol{X_2^T}\ \boldsymbol{M_1}\ \boldsymbol{X_2} ]^{-1}[\boldsymbol{X_2^T}\ \boldsymbol{M_1}\ \boldsymbol{y}]\]

Since \(\boldsymbol{M_1}\) is symmetric and idempotent, \(\boldsymbol{M_1} = \boldsymbol{M_1^T M_1}\).

\[\boldsymbol{b_2} = [\boldsymbol{X_2^T}\boldsymbol{M_1^T}\boldsymbol{M_1} \boldsymbol{X_2} ]^{-1}[\boldsymbol{X_2^T}\boldsymbol{M_1^T}\boldsymbol{M_1} \boldsymbol{y}]\]

Let \(\boldsymbol{M_1 X_2} = \boldsymbol{X_2^*}\) and \(\boldsymbol{M_1 y} = \boldsymbol{y^*}\)

\[\boldsymbol{b_2} = [\boldsymbol{{X_2^*}^T} \boldsymbol{X_2^*} ]^{-1}[\boldsymbol{{X_2^*}^T} \boldsymbol{y^*}]\]

13.6 FWL Theorem

The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell Theorem discusses a fundamental result in regression analysis.

In the linear least squares regression model of vector \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on two sets of variables, \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\), the subvector \(\boldsymbol{b_2}\) is the set of coefficients obtained when the residuals from a regression of \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) alone are regressed on the set of residuals obtained when each column of \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) is regressed on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\).

The least squares coefficients \(\boldsymbol{b_2}\) are obtained from regressing \(\boldsymbol{y^*}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_2^*}\).

\[\boldsymbol{b_2} = [\boldsymbol{{X_2^*}^T} \boldsymbol{X_2^*} ]^{-1}[\boldsymbol{{X_2^*}^T} \boldsymbol{y^*}]\]

But what are \(\boldsymbol{y^*}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2^*}\)?

\(\boldsymbol{y^*}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_2^*}\) are just the residuals obtained from regressing on the \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) variables.

The difference between regressing \(\boldsymbol{y}\) only on \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) is that the latter first regresses the dependent variable and all the \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) variables on the \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) variables and then regresses the resulting residuals on each other.

The smaller regression just regresses \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on the \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) variables.

13.7 What is the FWL Theorem?

The FWL theorem means that the multiple regression coefficient of any single variable can be obtained by first netting out the effect of the other variables in the regression model from the dependent variable and the independent variables.

In practice this means taking the residuals from regressing \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and regressing these residuals on the residuals from regressing \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\).

This process is known as partialing out or netting out the effect of \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\).

In other words, a multiple regression model partials out the influence of the the control variables.

Assume that our regression model was:

\[earnings = \beta_1 + \beta_2\ education + \beta_3\ age + \epsilon \]

We could first regress earnings on age and obtain the residuals. We could then regress education on age and obtain the residuals.

Regressing the residuals for earnings on the residuals for education would obtain the same coefficient as the full multiple regression model.

13.8 FWL Example

In the example below, we first regress reading scores on the percentage of students receiving free and reduced price lunch and average income in the school district.

\[ reading_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 lunch_i + \beta_2income_i + \epsilon_i \]

We want to demonstrate the FWL Theorem. We do this by

  • Regressing reading scores on income

  • Obtaining the residuals from the regression of reading on income

  • Regressing the lunch variable on income

  • Obtaining the residuals from the regression of lunch on income

  • Regressing the residuals from the regression of y on income on the residuals from lunch on income

When we regress y on income, the resulting residuals are the variation in y that is not explained by the income variable.

When we regress lunch on income, the resulting residuals are the variation in lunch that are not explained by the income variable.

We then regress the unexplained variation in y on the unexplained variation in lunch.

We have netted out the influence of income. The result should be the partial coefficient that represents the marginal influence of lunch on reading scores.

You can see in the table of regression estimates that the coefficient in the full model for lunch is also the coefficient the the lunch residuals in the regression of the y residuals on the lunch residuals.

As stated by the FWL Theorem, the coefficient obtained from the regression of \(\boldsymbol{M_1 y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{M_1 X_2}\) is the same as the coefficient from the multiple linear regression model on \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X}\).

This is why coefficients in multiple linear regression models are often referred to as partial regression coefficients.

13.9 Summary

The Orthogonal Partitioned Regression theorem states that if you regress \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on two sets of variables, \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\), then if (and only if) \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are orthogonal, you can obtain the coefficient vectors for \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) by regressing \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) separately.

What does this mean?

You can exclude the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) if these variables are irrelevant to the dependent variable or orthogonal to the other independent variables contained in \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\).

If the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are not irrelevant to the dependent variable, then the exclusion of these variables will change your estimates of \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\).

If the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) are not orthogonal to the variables in \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\), then the exclusion of these variables will change your estimates of \(\boldsymbol{b_1}\).

13.10 Orthogonal Example

In the R code, we create a large \(\boldsymbol{X}\) matrix and estimate \(\boldsymbol{b}\).

Note that the elements of \(\boldsymbol{X}\) are created randomly.

There is no underlying data generating process to suggest that the elements of \(\boldsymbol{X}\) violate the orthogonality assumption.

We partition \(\boldsymbol{X}\) into \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) and regress \(\boldsymbol{y}\) on \(\boldsymbol{X_1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{X_2}\) separately.

Comparing the full and partitioned regression results for the coefficients of the independent variables illustrates the partitioned orthogonal regression theorem.

13.12 Wrapping Up

If we omit independent variables that are not orthogonal to the other independent variables, the least squares coefficients are adversely impacted.

If we omit independent variables that are relevant to the dependent variable, the least squares coefficients are adversely impacted.

The least squares coefficients from the multiple regression model are partial regression coefficients.

A partial regression coefficient estimates the influence of the independent variable after the influence of other independent variables has been controlled for or netted out.

Hence, we preface our analysis with, “All else being equal” because we have (hopefully) controlled for the influence of other relevant variables of interest.
