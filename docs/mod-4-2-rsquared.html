16  R Squared

16.1 Introduction

In evaluating the performance of Ordinary Least Squares regressions, the total variation of the dependent variable is measured by the Total Sum of Squares (SST), which is calculated using the symmetric and idempotent centering matrix. This framework allows for the decomposition of SST into the Explained Sum of Squares (SSE) and the Residual Sum of Squares (SSR). From this decomposition, the coefficient of determination, \(R^2\) is derived to represent the proportion of total variation explained by the independent variables. However, \(R^2\) is only bounded between 0 and 1 when the model includes a constant term; without an intercept, the mathematical properties that simplify the SST decomposition fail to hold, making an unpredictable and unreliable metric.

A significant limitation of \(R^2\) is its mathematical property of non-decrease; adding any additional explanatory variable to a model will mechanically increase \(R^2\), even if the variable has no meaningful relationship with the dependent variable. To correct for this, the Adjusted \(R^2\) incorporates a penalty for each added regressor based on the degrees of freedom. Unlike the standard \(R^2\), the adjusted version will only increase if the new variable’s contribution to the model’s explanatory power is sufficient to offset the loss in degrees of freedom.

16.2 Total Sum of Squares for x

We can use \(\boldsymbol{M^0}\) to calculate the sum of the squared mean deviations of \(\boldsymbol{x}\).

For one variable \(\boldsymbol{x}\), the sum of squared mean deviations is:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 = (x_i - \bar{x})^T(x_i - \bar{x}) = (M^0 x)^T (M^0 x)\]

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 = (M^0 x)^T (M^0 x) = x^T M^{0T}M^0x\] Recall that \(M^0\) is symmetric or

\[M^{0T} = M^0\]

Recall that \(M^0\) is idempotent or

\[M^{0T}M^{0} = M^0\]

The sum of squared mean deviations for a single variable becomes:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 = x^T M^{0T}M^0x = x^T M^0 x\]

16.3 Total Sum of Squares for X

First, the transformation of the \(\boldsymbol{X}\) matrix into mean deviations is:

\[ \boldsymbol{M^0 X} \]

The sum of squares and cross-products of the mean deviations for \(\boldsymbol{X}\) is:

\[ \boldsymbol{(M^0 X)^T (M^0 X)} \]

\[ \boldsymbol{X^T M^{0T} M^0 X} \]

Since \(\boldsymbol{M^0}\) is symmetric and idempotent, this can be simplified to:

\[ \boldsymbol{X^T M^0 X} \]

While the single variable case for \(\boldsymbol{x^T M^0 x}\) yields a scalar which is the Total Sum of Squares for that variable, the matrix operation yields:

\[ \boldsymbol{X^T M^0 X} \sim (K \times K) \]

The diagonal elements of \(\boldsymbol{X^T M^0 X}\) are the sum of the squared deviations (SST) for each individual variable in the \(\boldsymbol{X}\) matrix. The off-diagonal elements represent the sum of the squared cross-products (SSCP) between different variables and are used to calculate covariances or:

\[ \boldsymbol{X^T M^0 X} = \begin{bmatrix} SST_{x1} & SSCP_{x1,x2} & \dots & SST_{x1,xK} \\ SSCP_{x2,x1} & SST_{x2} & \dots & SSCP_{x2,xK} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \dots & \vdots \\ SSCP_{xk,x1} & SST_{xk,x2} & \dots & SST_{xk} \\ \end{bmatrix} \]

However, recall that the first column of \(\boldsymbol{X}\) consists of 1’s and so every observation of \(\boldsymbol{x_1}\) is equal to its mean and thus the mean deviation of every observation in \(\boldsymbol{x_1}\) is 0. Also, since the off-diagonal elements represent the shared variation between variables (cross-products) and since \(\boldsymbol{x_1}\) does not vary, the sum of the squared cross-product between \(\boldsymbol{x_1}\) and any other variable is 0.

\[ \boldsymbol{X^T M^0 X} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & SST_{x2} & \dots & SSCP_{x2,xK} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \dots & \vdots \\ 0 & SST_{xk,x2} & \dots & SST_{xk} \\ \end{bmatrix} \]

16.4 Sum of Squares

For the dependent variable \(y\), the total sum of squares, \(SST_y\), is equal to the sum of squared mean deviations.

\[ SST_y = \boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y} \]

Given the relationship, \(\boldsymbol{y = Xb + e}\), we can pre-multiply by the centering matrix, \(\boldsymbol{M^0}\) or:

\[ \boldsymbol{y = Xb +e} \]

\[ \boldsymbol{M^0 y = M^0 Xb + M^0e} \]

If the model contains a constant term and the other assumptions hold, then the mean of the residuals is equal to zero, that is, \(\boldsymbol{\bar{e} = 0}\) this implies that:

\[ \boldsymbol{M^0 e = e} \]
\[ \boldsymbol{M^0 y = M^0 Xb + e} \]

And we can pre-multiply by \(\boldsymbol{y^T}\) to obtain:

\[ \boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y = (M^0 Xb +e)^T (M^{0}Xb + e)} \]

\[ \boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y = b^T X^T M^{0T}M^0Xb + b^T X^T M^{0T}e + e^TM^0Xb + e^Te} \]

Because the residuals, \(\boldsymbol{e}\) are orthogonal to the regressors, \(\boldsymbol{X}\) and, by extension, to the centered regressors, \(\boldsymbol{M^0X}\), the cross-product terms drop out. We can also rely on the fact that the centering matrix is symmetric and idempotent.

This leaves us with a fundamental identity:

\[ \boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y = b^T X^T M^0 Xb + e^T e} \]

Which can be expressed as:

\[ SST = SSE + SSR \]

That is, the total sum of squares of the dependent variable (SST) is equal to the explained sum of squares (SSE) and the residual sum of squares (SSR).

16.5 Decomposition in R

Building upon our previous examples, we can use simulated data to create our independent variables, population coefficients, and the dependent variable. We can estimate the least squares coefficients and construct the projection and residual maker matrices.

We then construct the \(\boldsymbol{M^0}\) matrix and use this matrix to estimate SST, SSE, and SSR.

The output table demonstrates the equality, that the total sum of squares can be decomposed into the explained sum of squares and the residual sum of squares.

16.6 R-Squared

The total sum of squares (SST) can be decomposed into the regression (or explained) sum of squares (SSE) and the residual sum of squares (SSR).

\[\boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y} = (\boldsymbol{b^T X^T M^0Xb}) + (\boldsymbol{e^T e})\] \[SST = SSE + SSR\]The coefficient of determination is a measure of how well a regression fits the data, where: \[R^2 = \frac{SSE}{SST} = \frac{(\boldsymbol{b^T X^T M^0Xb})}{\boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y}} = 1 - \frac{\boldsymbol{e^Te}}{\boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y}} = 1 - \frac{SSR}{SST}\]

\[ 0 \le R^2 \le 1 \]

When \(R^2 = 0\) the predicted values of \(y\) are \(\bar{y}\) so \(\boldsymbol{X}\) has no explanatory power.

When \(R^2 = 1\), the values of \(y\) and \(\boldsymbol{x}\) are such that the predicted values and actual values are the same.

16.7 R-Squared in R

First, we can show that \(\boldsymbol{M^0 e = e}\).

We can then calculate the total sum of squares, estimated sum of squares, and residual sum of squares.

\(R^2\) is equivalent whether obtained using the estimated or residual sum of squares.

One problem, however, is that one can continue to increase \(R^2\) by adding explanatory variables to the regression model.

16.8 R-Squared Increases in X

When we add a new variable \(\boldsymbol{z}\) to a regression already containing $, we can express the new residual sum of squares in terms of the old residual sum of squares or:

\[ u^T u = e^T e (1- r_{yz}^2) \] where \(r_{yz}^2\) is the squared correlation between \(\boldsymbol{y}\) and \(\boldsymbol{z}\) after both have been ‘purged’ of the influence of \(\boldsymbol{X}\).

Since \(r^2\) must be between 0 and 1, it follows that

\[ u^T u \le e^T e \] In terms of \(R^2\), this relationship simplifies to:

\[ R_{Xz}^2 = R_X^2 +(1-R_X^2)r_{yz}^2 \] What does this mean?

The new \(R^2\) will always be greater than or equal to the old \(R^2\).

In other words, adding an additional explanatory variable will never lower \(R^2\).

16.9 Constant Term Required

For \(R^2\) to be a reliable measure bounded between 0 and 1, the \(\boldsymbol{X}\) matrix must include a constant term (the column of 1’s).

With a constant term, we know:

\[ \boldsymbol{M^0 e = e} \] \[ \boldsymbol{e^T X = 0} \]Without a constant term, the mean of the residuals is not zero and the residuals are no longer orthogonal to the centered regressors.

Without an intercept, the cross-product term does not drop out. This breaks the SST=SSE+SSR identity, potentially leading to an “unpredictable” R2 that can even be negative.

16.10 Adjusted R-Squared

Given that one can increase \(R^2\) to 1 by adding independent variables, we can use adjusted \(R^2\) to examine the impact of the addition (or deletion) of an independent variable.

\[\bar{R^2} = 1 - \frac{\boldsymbol{e^Te}}{\boldsymbol{y^T M^0 y}}\frac{(n-1)}{(n-K)} = 1 - (1-R^2)\frac{(n-1)}{(n-K)} \] \(\bar{R^2}\) will fall (rise) when the variable \(x\) is deleted from the regression if the square of the t-ratio associated with this variable is greater (less) than 1.

In other words, the greater the statistical significance of a variable that is removed, the more significant the fall in adjusted \(R^2\).

The standard statistical output will include both \(R^2\) and \(\bar{R^2}\) in the output.
